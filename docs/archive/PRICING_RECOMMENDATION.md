## The honest read

You are priced at parity with a competitor who ships more, and you have
zero paying customers and zero proof either price point works. That's
not really a "what should the number be" problem yet — it's a "does
anyone say yes at any number" problem. Whatever you pick below, the
actual test is still the same one from the last conversation: real
outreach, real conversations, real objections. Pricing analysis doesn't
substitute for that.

## Three options

### (a) Founding-member launch price: R249–299/month, locked for life

**The case for it:** You have no case studies, no reviews, no brand
recognition, and a narrower feature set than the competitor charging the
same price. A meaningfully lower price is the one lever you can pull
immediately, without building anything, to make "why would I pick the
newer, smaller tool" easier to answer — the answer becomes "because it's
cheaper and I lock that in forever." A locked-for-life rate also gives
early adopters a genuine reason to say yes *now* rather than "wait and
see," and gives you something concrete to use as a referral incentive
("bring another school, keep your rate").

**The case against it:** R249–299 barely clears your own infrastructure
cost margin at very low volume, and "locked for life" is a permanent
commitment — every founding customer you sign at R249 is R250+/month of
margin you never recover from them, for as long as they stay. If the
product gets meaningfully better (parent communication, attendance) and
you want to raise prices for new customers later, your best, longest-
tenured customers are the ones paying the least. You'd be trading
short-term conversion for long-term unit economics you can't undo per
customer.

### (b) Hold R499 — and actually justify it

**The case for it:** R499 is not an unreasonable price for what the
product does, in isolation — it's genuinely useful, it saves real time,
and R499/month is small relative to what a single family's monthly fees
already are (your own pricing page framing — "one paying family a month
covers it" — is a legitimate value argument regardless of what
Koonection charges). Holding the price also avoids the founding-member
trap above, and if outreach conversations show people saying yes at
R499 without much hesitation, that's a strong signal you could safely
charge *more* later, not less.

**The case against it:** You're asking a prospect to pay the same as a
competitor who visibly does more, with zero social proof that you're a
credible option. In a market where the buyer is comparing 2-3 tools in
one evening on a phone, "same price, less product, no reviews" is a hard
sell before you've had a single validating conversation. Holding R499
only makes sense if you're confident the objection "Koonection does more
for the same price" won't kill most conversations — and right now you
don't know that, because no conversations have happened yet.

### (c) Add an "under 25 children" entry tier

**The case for it:** A meaningful share of South African preschools are
genuinely small (owner-operated, 10-20 children) — R499/month is a much
bigger share of a 15-child crèche's revenue than a 60-child one's. A
lower entry tier could unlock a segment that would otherwise never
convert at flat R499, without discounting the price for larger schools
who can clearly afford it.

**The case against it — and this is the important one:** tiering
directly breaks the cleanest thing about your current pricing page,
which is "no tiers, nothing locked behind a higher price" — a real,
legitimate differentiator against Koonection's own "no per-learner fees"
positioning (they compete on flat-price simplicity too). The moment you
add a tier, you also create real product work: you need an actual child
count enforced somewhere, upgrade/downgrade logic, and a new "which tier
am I on" support burden — all before you know if the *segment* you're
trying to reach even wants the product at any price. Tiering is a
mature-product move to capture more of a validated market; you don't
have a validated market yet.

## What I'd actually flag as the real decision

Between (a) and (b): the honest tie-breaker isn't a pricing model, it's
what you're optimizing for right now. If you need signal fast — "will
anyone say yes at all" — (a) removes a real objection and costs you
nothing until you have paying customers to lose margin on. If you'd
rather learn whether R499 itself is viable before you commit to a
lifetime discount you can't undo, (b) is the cleaner experiment, and
you can always retroactively offer early customers a discount later if
outreach stalls — that's reversible; a "locked for life" promise isn't.

(c) is the one I'd actively avoid *for now* — not because small schools
don't matter, but because it solves a problem you haven't confirmed you
have yet (that price, not product gap, is what's actually stopping
small schools from saying yes), and it costs you your cleanest
positioning line to find out.

**Your next move:** whichever number you pick, the next real step is
still the same 20-30-school outreach list from the earlier advisor
conversation — pricing theory doesn't replace that, and no version of
this document can tell you what a real South African preschool owner
will actually pay without you asking one.
